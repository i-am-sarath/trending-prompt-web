The packaging conceit is what makes this prompt reliable: instead of asking the model to invent a photorealistic toy sculpt from scratch, which tends to produce uncanny plastic skin and melted proportions, it asks for a photograph of an already-packaged product. That is a category every image model has seen thousands of times in e-commerce and retail catalog data, so PACKAGING and LIGHTING are the load-bearing clauses here, not SUBJECT.

Naming the vacuum-formed blister bubble, the printed cardboard backing card, and a generic logo placeholder gives the model a concrete object to render rather than an abstract toy version of a person, which is why identity carries through more reliably than in plain figurine prompts. Keeping the brand and figure name generic, and swapping the figure name for whoever is in your reference photo, is what keeps this reproducible without tripping trademark-related refusals.

The accessory row is the easiest lever to customize: match it to whatever actually reads in your reference photo, a laptop bag instead of a purse, a skateboard, a musical instrument, and the card feels bespoke instead of templated. LIGHTING is the second lever worth protecting: clean softbox product lighting is what sells the photographed-on-a-shelf illusion, so swapping it for anything moodier makes the packaging read as fake.
