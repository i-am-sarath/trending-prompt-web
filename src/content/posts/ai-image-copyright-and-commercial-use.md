There are two separate questions people run together, and untangling them clears up most of the confusion.

1. **What does the tool let me do?** A contract question, answered by the terms of service.
2. **Do I own any rights in the output?** A copyright question, answered by law, not by the tool.

A tool can grant you every right it has and still leave you with an image nobody can own. Both questions matter, and they have different answers in different places.

This is general background, not legal advice. Rules differ by jurisdiction and are moving quickly. For anything commercially significant, take advice from a qualified lawyer where you operate.

## Question one: what the tool allows

Each generator sets its own terms, and they differ in ways that matter.

**Midjourney** grants asset ownership to paid subscribers under its terms of service, subject to conditions. Two are worth knowing: output generated on the free tier has historically been made available under a Creative Commons non-commercial licence rather than owned outright, and companies above a revenue threshold are required to hold a higher tier. Terms have been revised more than once, so read the current version.

**OpenAI** assigns ownership of output to the user under its terms, subject to compliance with its usage policies. Content that violates those policies is a different matter, and the assignment does not protect you from other people's rights in what you generated.

**Stable Diffusion** is not one thing. The base models carry their own licences — the OpenRAIL family attaches use restrictions rather than a simple grant — and community checkpoints on model-sharing sites each carry their own terms, some of which prohibit commercial use outright. If you are using a downloaded checkpoint commercially, read that specific model's licence. This is the most commonly ignored risk in the whole field.

**Adobe Firefly** is positioned around training data Adobe has rights to, and Adobe offers enterprise indemnification. If indemnity matters to your organisation more than image quality does, this is the reason it exists.

The practical point: **the licence attaches to the tool and the tier, not to the image.** An image on this site generated in Midjourney is governed by Midjourney's terms as they apply to you, not by anything this site can grant.

## Question two: whether copyright exists at all

Separately from what the tool permits, there is the question of whether the resulting image attracts copyright protection. In several jurisdictions, the answer is largely no.

**The United States** requires human authorship. The Copyright Office has consistently refused registration for material generated by AI without sufficient human creative contribution. The well-known case of a graphic novel led to registration covering the human-authored text and the selection and arrangement of images, while excluding the images themselves. Federal courts have upheld the human-authorship requirement in related litigation.

The Office's guidance has developed the position that prompts alone generally do not supply the necessary authorship — because the prompt does not determine the expressive detail of the output the way a brushstroke does. Human contribution that *can* count includes substantial modification of generated material, and the creative selection and arrangement of generated elements into a larger work.

**The United Kingdom** has an unusual provision for computer-generated works with no human author, assigning authorship to the person who made the arrangements necessary for creation, with a shorter term. Its application to modern generative systems is contested and has been under review.

**The European Union** generally requires a work to be the author's own intellectual creation, which points in a similar direction to the US position. The EU's AI Act adds transparency obligations around generated content, which is a disclosure question rather than an ownership one.

**China** has produced court decisions finding copyright in AI-assisted images where the user's input was sufficiently creative — a notably different direction from the US, and a reminder that "AI images can't be copyrighted" is a statement about particular jurisdictions rather than a universal rule.

The practical consequence for most people: **an unmodified generated image is probably not protectable in the US and several other markets.** You can use it. You may not be able to stop anyone else from using it too.

## What this means in practice

**For social content, blog images, mock-ups and internal work:** the copyright question rarely matters. Nobody is going to copy your blog header, and if they do, little is lost.

**For a logo or brand identity:** it matters a great deal. An unprotectable logo is a weak asset. Redraw generated concepts as original vector artwork so that human authorship is genuine and substantial — which is also, as the [logo guide](/blog/ai-logo-design-prompts) argues, better design practice regardless.

**For anything you intend to license, sell or enforce:** add substantial human authorship. Composite, paint over, edit, arrange into a larger work. Keep records of what you did — layered files, version history, working notes. If protection is ever tested, evidence of the human contribution is the whole case.

**For client work:** say what is generated. Many clients now ask, some contracts prohibit it, and discovering the answer after delivery damages relationships. Some industries — publishing, advertising, stock — have specific and evolving requirements.

## Risks that have nothing to do with ownership

Even where a tool grants you rights, other people's rights survive.

**Trademarks.** Generating a soft drink can that resembles a well-known brand does not license you to use it. Trademark law is independent of copyright and of the tool's terms.

**Personality rights.** Generating a recognisable likeness of a real person for commercial purposes engages publicity and personality rights in many jurisdictions, quite apart from copyright, and is usually prohibited by the tools' own policies.

**Substantial similarity to existing works.** Models can produce output closely resembling specific training images, particularly for heavily-represented subjects. If a generated image is substantially similar to an existing protected work, the fact that a model produced it is not a defence.

**Ongoing litigation over training data.** Several suits concerning whether training on copyrighted material without permission is lawful remain unresolved in various jurisdictions. Outcomes could affect downstream users, which is precisely why some vendors offer indemnification.

## Disclosure

Separate from law, disclosure is becoming standard expectation:

- Several platforms label AI-generated media, sometimes automatically via provenance metadata.
- The EU AI Act includes transparency requirements for generated content.
- Advertising standards bodies in multiple markets have taken positions on misleading imagery.
- Stock libraries, publishers and competitions have their own rules, many of which require declaration and some of which prohibit submission.

The reliable approach is to disclose by default. It costs nothing when it is not required and protects you when it is. Every image on this site is described as generated output, along with the tool that made it, for exactly that reason.

## A checklist before commercial use

1. **Read the current terms** of the tool and the tier you are on. Not a summary; the terms.
2. **If using a downloaded checkpoint,** read that model's licence specifically.
3. **Check for trademarks and likenesses** in the image, including on incidental background objects.
4. **Reverse image search** anything important, to catch close resemblance to an existing work.
5. **Add human authorship** if you need protection — edit, composite, redraw — and keep the working files.
6. **Record your process:** tool, version, date, prompt, what you changed.
7. **Disclose** where the audience could reasonably assume a photograph.
8. **Get advice** for anything with real money attached.

## Why prompts are a different case

One clarification, since this is a site full of prompts. Short functional text strings of the kind used to instruct a model attract little or no copyright protection in most jurisdictions — they are too short and too functional to constitute a protected work.

That is why prompt text on this site is free to use, adapt and republish without attribution, as set out in the [terms](/terms). The images are a different question, governed by the terms of whichever tool produced them, and worth checking before you reuse one.

## Common questions

### If the tool says I own the output, doesn't that settle it?

No, and this is the most common misunderstanding in the field. The tool can only grant what it has: a contractual promise not to assert its own claims over your output. It cannot create copyright where the law says none exists, and it cannot license you rights belonging to third parties. You can hold a full grant from a generator and still have an image that is unprotectable in your jurisdiction and that infringes somebody's trademark.

### How much editing counts as substantial human authorship?

There is no bright line, which is unhelpful but honest. The direction of travel in US guidance is that protection attaches to the human contribution — significant modification, or the creative selection and arrangement of generated elements — rather than to the generated material itself. Practically: the more of the final image reflects decisions you made and executed, the stronger your position. Keep layered files and version history as evidence.

### Do I need to disclose that an image is AI-generated?

Legally it depends on jurisdiction and context; the EU AI Act includes transparency obligations, and advertising standards bodies in several markets have taken positions on misleading imagery. Practically, disclose by default. It costs nothing where it is not required, protects you where it is, and platforms increasingly detect and label generated media automatically through provenance metadata whether you declare it or not.

### Is it safe to use a community model I downloaded?

Only after reading its licence. Checkpoints on model-sharing sites carry their own terms, and a meaningful number restrict or prohibit commercial use, or impose conditions inherited from the base model. This is the most frequently ignored risk in the entire workflow, because downloading a file feels like acquiring a tool rather than entering a licence. Check the specific model page, not the base architecture.
