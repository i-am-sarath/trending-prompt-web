Turning yourself into a toy has been one of the longest-running AI image trends, and it keeps coming back because it works for everyone: you get a recognisable, shareable version of yourself without the uncanny feeling of a straight AI portrait. There are two main versions of the trend, and they need quite different prompts.

1. **The desk figurine** — a painted 1/7 scale collectible standing on a clear acrylic base, with its 3D model on a monitor and its retail box beside it.
2. **The blister-pack action figure** — a boxed toy in a clear plastic bubble on a printed card, with tiny accessories.

Both prompts are in the archive with the images they produced. This guide explains how each one works and how to fix the usual problems.

## Version 1: the desk collectible figurine

![A 1/7 scale painted figurine of a man in a beige trench coat on a clear acrylic base, with its 3D sculpt on a monitor and its retail box beside it](/uploads/desk-collectible-figurine-with-box.jpg)

**Copy the full prompt:** [Desk Collectible Figurine With Box](/prompts/desk-collectible-figurine-with-box/)

This version sells the toy through context. Three objects appear together — the figure, the grey 3D sculpt of the same figure on screen, and the box with its illustration — and asking the model to keep all three consistent forces it to commit to a single clear design. That is why the face in this version tends to stay more stable than in a plain "make me a figurine" prompt.

The details that matter most:

- **`1/7 scale`** tells the model how big the figure is next to a keyboard and monitor. Without a scale, figures drift between palm-sized and doll-sized from one generation to the next.
- **`round, clear acrylic base with no text`** is the single most recognisable detail of premium collectible figures. Drop it and the figure often reads as a tiny person standing on a desk.
- **A fictional box brand.** Models will happily print a real toy company's logo. Saying `generic, fictional toy-brand style with no real logos` keeps it out, though you may still get garbled lettering on the box — check before posting.

## Version 2: the blister-pack action figure

**Copy the full prompt:** [Collectible Action Figure — Blister Pack Edition](/prompts/collectible-action-figure--blister-pack-edition/)

This one sells the toy through its packaging instead. Image models have seen thousands of e-commerce photos of boxed toys, so asking for *a photo of a packaged product* is much more reliable than asking for *a toy version of a person*. The vacuum-formed bubble, cardboard backing card and softbox product lighting do most of the work.

The easiest thing to personalise is the **accessory row**. Match it to something that is actually yours — a laptop bag, a camera, a skateboard, a coffee cup — and the card feels made for you instead of templated.

## Which one should you use?

| | Desk figurine | Blister pack |
|---|---|---|
| Looks like | A collector's photo of a new figure | A product listing for a toy |
| Face stability | Higher | Slightly lower behind the plastic |
| Best for | Profile pictures, portfolio posts | Funny posts, gifts, team intros |
| Text risk | Box lettering | Card name and logo |

## Getting your face to survive the toy treatment

The most common complaint with both versions is that the figure is clearly a person in a trench coat, but not clearly *you*. Things that help:

- **Use a full-body photo or a waist-up photo with your outfit visible.** The model needs to see your clothes to rebuild them in miniature.
- **Keep the face large in the reference.** If you are small in the frame, crop the photo first.
- **Name your signature features.** Add a line such as `keep his round glasses, short beard and side-parted hair` — explicit features survive stylisation far better than "preserve identity".
- **Ask for realism in the paint, not in the skin.** Figures look right when the face is *painted* PVC. If yours looks like a shrunken real person, add `stylised collectible sculpt, painted features, not a miniature human`.

## Common failures and fixes

**The monitor shows a different character.** Add `the 3D model on the monitor is the exact same figure, same pose and outfit, untextured grey`.

**Real brand logos appear on the monitor or box.** Image models often add a real monitor brand. Retouch it out, or add `unbranded monitor` to the desk scene.

**The figure has extra fingers.** Ask for a simpler pose: `arms relaxed at the sides`. Fewer visible fingers means fewer errors.

**It looks like a 3D render, not a photo.** Strengthen the photographic language: `shot on a phone by a collector, natural window light, slight noise, shallow depth of field`.

## FAQ

**What is the AI figurine trend?**
Uploading a photo of yourself to an image model like Gemini or ChatGPT and asking it to render you as a collectible figure, usually shown with its box and the 3D model it was "made" from.

**Which AI is best for the figurine trend?**
Both Gemini and ChatGPT handle it. The desk-figurine example above was made in Gemini. If one tool drifts on your face, try the same prompt in the other.

**Can I sell figures based on these images?**
The image is a mock-up, not a product. If you turn it into a real figure or use it commercially, read our guide on [AI image copyright and commercial use](/blog/ai-image-copyright-and-commercial-use/), and never put a real brand's logo on the packaging.
